## What cheap reseller hosting actually costs right now

The honest baseline: a September 2026 price comparison covering 49 reseller hosts under $15/mo found a median entry price of **$5.67/mo** — and, among the 11 hosts that publish a renewal rate, a median renewal of **$14.99/mo**. Only three of the 49 never raise the price.

So "cheap" in this market usually means "cheap for twelve months." Hosts buy your signup with a deep first-term discount, then reprice at renewal, where their real margins live. That's how a plan can open at $4.99 and renew at $29.99 — exact numbers below.

It also means the entry price is the least useful figure on any pricing page. When two plans both advertise $5-ish, the one with the flat renewal is often 40–60% cheaper over three years. Keep that lens for everything that follows.

## Hidden costs that quietly eat a reseller's margin

The monthly fee is rarely where a reseller business gets expensive. These line items are.

**Client account caps.** Marketing says "unlimited websites"; the binding limit is almost always the number of cPanel accounts. Asura Hosting's entry cPanel plan, for example, bundles 25 GB of NVMe storage but only 5 client accounts — you're full at five clients no matter how light their sites are. Compare plans by account count, not gigabytes.

**Billing software.** WHMCS automates provisioning and invoicing, and most serious resellers eventually want it. It's usually a paid add-on: Asura sells the WHMCS license at $9.99/mo (Blesta at $2.99/mo), which can double the running cost of a budget reseller plan. Manual invoicing through PayPal works fine under ten clients; just know the automation carries its own subscription.

**Panel economics.** cPanel licenses cost hosts money per account, which is precisely why cheap plans cap accounts. Hosts dodge this two ways: DirectAdmin, which is cheaper to license (Asura's DirectAdmin plans start at $3.29 vs $4.99 for the cPanel equivalent), or in-house panels. ScalaHosting built its reseller offer around SPanel, its own cPanel replacement, allowing unlimited accounts with no per-account licensing at $11.21/mo — though renewal jumps to $39.95/mo, and clients raised on cPanel will face a learning curve.

**Overselling temptation.** Budget hosts typically enable overselling, so you can sell more total resources than physically exist, because most sites use a fraction of their allocation. It works — until a client's store gets a traffic spike and eats the disk. Know your real headroom before pricing packages.

## When reseller hosting is overkill

Reseller hosting is overkill if you host a handful of sites that you alone control. Four client brochure sites fit on one multi-site shared plan costing $3–5/mo, or on one of the cheap KVM VPS deals budget hosts (RackNerd, famously) run for $20–30 a year. Neither gives clients their own login — but if clients never touch the hosting, you don't need one.

Reseller hosting earns its monthly fee in three situations: clients need their own cPanel logins and you want to stop being the bottleneck for every password reset; you want per-client resource isolation so one misbehaving site can't drag down the others; or you're selling hosting as a branded product with your own packages and pricing. Private nameservers, white-label panels, and per-account limits exist for exactly this.

There's also a middle path worth knowing about. Hostwinds structures reselling around their standard shared hosting accounts — you buy at retail with volume discounts up to 20% as you grow. Simpler to understand, thinner margins per account.

One expectation to set honestly: reselling isn't passive income. Your clients call you first when email bounces at 2 a.m., and the host's support answers the person who pays them — you. If that trade doesn't appeal, a plain multi-site plan serves your clients just as well for less money.

## A five-minute checklist before you order

Work through this in order. It takes less time than migrating away from a bad choice later.

1. **Count client accounts** — present, plus an 18-month projection. This sets your tier more reliably than storage numbers.
2. **Find the renewal price and compute the three-year total.** If a host doesn't publish it, treat that omission as a data point in itself.
3. **Verify white-label specifics:** private nameservers, no host branding inside the client panel, freedom to build your own packages and set per-client limits.
4. **Match the panel to your clients.** cPanel is the safe default — it's what tutorials and client expectations are built around. DirectAdmin or SPanel can cut costs if your clients genuinely don't care.
5. **Ask two questions before paying:** what migration help looks like if you're moving existing client sites (ScalaHosting and SiteGround include free migrations; that's not universal at the budget end), and what the refund terms are for the exact product you're buying — budget hosts often exclude promotional products from refunds.

## FAQ

**How much should I budget for cheap reseller hosting?**
Plan around the renewal rate: the current medians are $5.67/mo to sign up and $14.99/mo to renew. On flat-pricing lineups like RackNerd's ($4.99–$24.99/mo), the number you see is the number you keep paying.

**Is reseller hosting still profitable?**
Yes, at small scale, with modest pricing. Say you sell 12 client accounts at $9/mo each on RackNerd's Advanced plan ($8.99/mo): $108 gross, roughly $99/mo after payment processing fees, against a cost that stays flat. The usual constraints are your support time and client churn, rarely the hosting fee itself.

**cPanel, DirectAdmin, or an alternative panel?**
cPanel if your clients expect mainstream tooling — it's the compatibility default. DirectAdmin if cutting licensing cost matters more, which is why DirectAdmin plans run a dollar or two cheaper at comparable hosts. In-house panels remove licensing fees and account caps entirely, but add a learning curve and, in ScalaHosting's case, a steep renewal.

**Do I need WHMCS to start?**
No. Under roughly ten clients, manual invoicing through PayPal or Stripe costs nothing and works fine. WHMCS becomes worth its ~$10/mo add-on fee once provisioning and invoice-chasing eat real hours.
